A retailer tested two emails against no email. One email clearly earned its keep. This readout is built the way experimentation platforms work: checks first, a metric for every question, no reading the result early, and a decision at the end.

02 · Before any result

Was the split fair?

If the three groups differ in size or in who they contain, every number after this is suspect. Bars show group sizes; dots show how far each group drifts from the no-email group on things measured before the email went out.

03 · When to stop

Checking a test every day makes it lie, unless the stats allow for it

Most teams look at their test daily. With the usual p-value, each look is another chance of a false alarm. An always-valid p-value is built to be checked as often as you like. Grey: the usual p-value. Colour: always-valid. A call is safe once the coloured line drops below the 0.05 line.

04 · Guardrail

Who does it work for?

Change in spend per customer for each group, against no email. The guardrail fails if any group is clearly worse off. The dashed line is the most loss we'd accept (−$1 per customer).

05 · Speed-up check

Why this test couldn't be made faster

CUPED shortens tests by using each customer's past behaviour to cancel out noise. It only works when the past predicts the outcome. Here, last year's spend barely predicts spend in the two weeks after the email.

06 · The decision

Which email, to whom, and is it worth the send?

Each option was chosen on one random half of customers and scored on the other half, so the targeted option isn't graded on the data that picked it. Move the sliders to your own margin and email cost. The figures are your assumptions, not data.

Metric registry and data checks

Every metric is defined once in metrics.yml in the repo. Change a definition, rerun the analysis, and every chart and the memo update together.
